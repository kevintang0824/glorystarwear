Technical construction guide

Activewear Seam and Stitch Construction Guide

Translate flatlock, coverstitch, overlock, binding, elastic attachment, and reinforcement ideas into controlled tech-pack callouts and verifiable samples.

Download Seam SpecificationOpen Tech Pack GuideDiscuss Construction

Direct answer

How should activewear seams and stitches be specified?

Specify the garment area and seam purpose, material code and stretch direction, seam type, stitch reference, thread, needle, density method, seam allowance, reinforcement, finish, body-contact requirement, sample check, test or wear condition, acceptance rule, approved sample ID, and bulk verification point. Flatlock, coverstitch, and overlock describe different constructions or operations and should not be used as interchangeable appearance labels.

By GloryStarWear product development teamPublished and updated August 29, 2026For leggings, sports bras, compression layers, running wear, and teamwearReview fabric planning

Separate seam from stitch

The seam describes how material parts are arranged and joined; the stitch describes the interlacing or looping structure used to form the join or finish.

Start with function

State whether the operation joins panels, finishes an edge, attaches elastic, secures a pocket, controls bulk, reinforces stress, or manages next-to-skin comfort.

Connect the fabric

Evaluate stretch direction, recovery, thickness, surface, perforation risk, edge behavior, elastic interaction, thread and intended wearing condition together.

Approve evidence

Use a dated sample and written check for appearance, extension, recovery, strength where applicable, comfort, wash, movement, and production repeatability.

Construction vocabulary

Flatlock, coverstitch, and overlock are not interchangeable

Machine names, local trade terms, visual references, and stitch terminology can differ. Pair the written reference with a cross-section or callout, material assembly, and approved physical result.

TermWhat it helps describeWhat it does not prove by itself
Seam typeThe arrangement and relationship of the material plies or edges in the stitched assembly.The exact stitch, thread, density, allowance, elasticity, comfort, strength, or finished appearance.
Stitch type or classHow threads interlace, interloop or intraloop to form a stitch structure.The complete seam assembly, machine settings, material response, or suitability for a product location.
Overlock or sergingAn edge-neatening or joining route commonly associated with looped stitches that wrap a cut edge.A universal activewear join, a flat seam, or guaranteed extension and recovery on every fabric.
CoverstitchA stitch route commonly used for hems, elastic or binding operations, with needle threads visible on one side and cover or looper formation on the other.The number of needles, cover configuration, seam purpose, thread, density, tension, stretch or approved appearance.
Flatlock or flatseamA family of relatively low-profile joining constructions often considered for stretch garments and skin-contact zones.That two suppliers mean the same seam, stitch class, width, security, coverage, feel, or strength.
Decorative topstitchA visible row or group of stitches used for appearance, stabilization, edge control, attachment, or reinforcement.That it carries structural load or replaces a specified joining seam.

Record the complete seam system

A stitch number alone is rarely a complete manufacturing instruction. Connect the operation to material, location, equipment feasibility, sample evidence, and an acceptance rule.

Development workflow

Verify the seam from intent through bulk production

  1. Map every construction location

    Mark joins, hems, openings, edges, elastic, bindings, pockets, closures, labels, reinforcements, intersections, high-load zones, and skin-contact areas on consistent technical views.

  2. Define the functional need

    State the load, stretch, recovery, profile, comfort, appearance, coverage, drainage, ventilation, abrasion, security, and repair requirements for each location.

  3. Identify the exact material assembly

    Use coded face, lining, mesh, elastic, binding, tape and thread references with orientation and approved alternatives. Do not choose a seam from a generic fabric description.

  4. Request a feasible construction proposal

    Ask the technical designer and manufacturer to return the seam, stitch, thread, needle, equipment, allowance, reinforcement, expected result, limitations, and any conflicting callout.

  5. Build a representative sample

    Where risk is high, test the actual material assembly and operation before or within a complete garment. Mark the sample ID and settings or inputs that must remain controlled.

  6. Review the product in use

    Combine visual inspection with relevant extension, recovery, wash, movement, skin-contact, load, and product-specific wear conditions. A flat table review cannot reproduce every activewear demand.

  7. Record failures by location and condition

    Distinguish thread break, skipped stitch, seam opening, slippage, fabric rupture, needle damage, waviness, tunneling, bulk, edge exposure, abrasion, unraveling, or appearance mismatch.

  8. Release one approved revision

    Update drawings, callouts, BOM, construction table, measurements where affected, sample comments, acceptance rules, approved sample ID, deviation log, and release authority together.

  9. Verify first output and bulk

    Compare setup and first output with the approved reference, inspect risk operations in line, retain defect examples, and connect final release to the order and current specification.

Scope limitation

Match every test method to the product and material

ASTM D1683/D1683M-22 is scoped to woven fabrics and states that it does not predict actual seam wear performance. Many fitted activewear products use knits. A qualified laboratory, technical team and buyer should agree applicable methods, specimens, conditioning, directions, care states, sample sizes, acceptance rules and product wear checks before relying on a result.

FAQ

Activewear seam and stitch questions

What is the difference between flatlock and coverstitch?

Flatlock or flatseam commonly refers to a relatively low-profile joining construction, while coverstitch commonly refers to stitch formations used for operations such as hems, bindings, or elastic attachment. Supplier terminology can vary, so specify seam purpose, material arrangement, stitch reference, visible sides, width, thread, and an approved sample instead of relying on the name alone.

What is the best seam for activewear?

There is no universal best seam. The choice depends on garment area, function, load direction, material and trim assembly, stretch and recovery, fit, skin contact, thickness, appearance, equipment, thread, needle, care, test evidence, wear condition, repair needs, and production control.

How do I specify activewear seams in a tech pack?

For each location, record purpose, material codes and orientation, seam type, stitch reference, row or needle arrangement, thread, needle, density measurement method, allowance, reinforcement, finish, diagram, approved sample ID, test or wear condition, acceptance rule, and bulk inspection point.

Why do activewear seams pop, wave, skip, or chafe?

Possible causes include a mismatch between fabric behavior and seam system, fit or load, stitch formation, thread, needle, density, tension, feed, allowance, reinforcement, edge handling, local thickness, operator method, wear condition, or uncontrolled substitution. Record the exact failure and conditions before changing one variable.

How should seams be verified before bulk production?

Review a representative material assembly or garment for construction accuracy, appearance, extension, recovery, relevant strength or failure behavior, skin comfort, movement, wash, decoration or print interaction, and product-specific use. Release the written revision and approved physical reference together, then compare first output and bulk risk operations against them.

Need a construction review?

Send the garment area, material code, technical view, current callout, and failure or use condition.

Include the size range, fit intent, quantity, target market, sample revision, artwork or print interaction, and the questions that must be answered before approval.
